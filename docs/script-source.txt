[2] INTRODUCTION — INEQUALITY  (~1:00)

Recently, Santé publique France found that 18.2 percent of adults aged 18 to 75
smoke every day. But this isn't spread evenly. Look at this graph: 25 percent of
manual workers are daily smokers — compared to only 12 percent in the higher
socioeconomic group. This is a well-known inequality. But what about other
factors that shape this picture just as strongly — factors we don't usually
talk about?

Anchors: 18.2% daily smoking / 25% vs 12% / other factors

----------------------------------------------------------------

[3] INTRODUCTION — ADVERSE CHILDHOOD EXPERIENCES  (~1:00)

Other factors shape this picture too — such as mental health conditions,
migration background, or experiences of adversity in childhood. Adverse
childhood experiences are negative experiences occurring before the age of
eighteen, which affect children's development, with consequences on mental and
physical health in adulthood. But the mechanisms linking this type of trauma to
psychoactive substance use remain poorly documented at the population level —
specifically for an understudied form of maltreatment: childhood sexual
violence.

Anchors: mental health, migration, adversity / ACE definition / mechanisms poorly documented

----------------------------------------------------------------

[4] DEFINITION & PREVALENCE  (~1:00)

Childhood sexual violence is defined as any sexual act imposed on a person
under the age of eighteen, through coercion, manipulation, or abuse of a
position of authority. A recent systematic review found that 11 percent of
children reported sexual harassment, 9 percent contact sexual violence, and 6
percent forced intercourse. And across the literature, smoking prevalence is
consistently reported to be higher among survivors of childhood sexual
violence, compared to those who were not exposed.

Anchors: coercion, authority / 11-9-6 percent / higher smoking vs not exposed

----------------------------------------------------------------

[5] GAPS & OBJECTIVE  (~1:30)

But how well is this association actually studied? A few gaps stand out.
First, sexual violence is often combined with other adverse experiences into a
single cumulative ACE score, rather than studied on its own. Second, existing
studies often focus on specific, high-risk populations — such as migrants,
incarcerated individuals, military personnel, or children in institutional
care — usually with small sample sizes. Third, much of the evidence dates back
to studies from around nineteen ninety to twenty ten, with limited recent
replication. It's also worth noting that since the MeToo movement went viral
in 2017, disclosure and reporting of sexual violence have become significantly
less stigmatized — which is one more reason older data may not fully reflect
today's reporting patterns. Finally, tobacco is rarely studied as a standalone
outcome — it's usually pooled with other behaviors, such as substance use,
obesity, or physical inactivity. So, the objective of this study is: is
exposure to childhood sexual violence associated with smoking status in
adulthood?

Anchors: combined, not on its own / high-risk, small samples / 1990-2010, no replication /
MeToo 2017 — less stigmatized / pooled with other behaviors / objective question

----------------------------------------------------------------

[6] STUDY POPULATION  (~0:45)

To answer this question, I used data from Constances — one of the largest
cohorts in France, with around 220,000 participants aged 18 to 69 at
inclusion. Constances allows us to track health over time, and it collects
rich data across many domains — lifestyle, environment, occupation, and more.

Anchors: Constances / 220,000 — 18 to 69 / health over time

----------------------------------------------------------------

[7] EXPOSURE  (~1:00)

Childhood sexual violence was assessed using three questions from the
Constances questionnaire. The first asked whether someone tried to get the
child to touch them sexually. The second asked whether someone subjected the
child to sexual touching. And the third asked about forced intercourse. Based
on the type and frequency of exposure, we built five categories: no exposure,
sexual assault — once or repeated — and forced intercourse — once or repeated.

Anchors: three questions / touch / subjected / forced intercourse / five categories

----------------------------------------------------------------

[8] OUTCOMES  (~0:45)

We looked at tobacco use two ways. First, how long people have smoked
overall — grouped into less than one year, or one year or more. And
separately, whether they're still smoking today: never smoked, ex-smoker, or
current smoker.

Anchors: two ways / duration: less/more than 1yr / status: never/ex/current

----------------------------------------------------------------

[9] STATISTICAL ANALYSES  (~1:00)

We used two types of models. For smoking status, we ran two separate binary
comparisons — never-smokers versus current smokers, and ex-smokers versus
current smokers. For duration of smoking, we used a multinomial model,
comparing less than one year, or one year or more, to never having smoked. All
models were adjusted for age, origin, education level, parents'
socio-occupational category, and other adverse childhood experiences. And all
analyses were run separately for women and men.

Anchors: binary — smoking status / multinomial — duration / adjusted for... / separately — women, men

----------------------------------------------------------------

[10] POPULATION DESCRIPTION  (~1:15)

Our final sample includes just over 102,000 participants. As we saw before,
women are strongly overrepresented among those exposed — 76 percent, compared
to 52 percent among the non-exposed. Other adverse childhood experiences are
also more frequent in the exposed group. Looking at tobacco use, exposed
participants show a higher proportion of current smokers, and a lower
proportion of never-smokers, compared to non-exposed participants. Age at
smoking initiation is very similar between the two groups. Finally, for years
of smoking, both categories are more frequent among those exposed: 2.2 percent
report less than one year, compared to 1.8 percent among the non-exposed — and
55 percent report one year or more, compared to 46 percent.

Anchors: 102,000 / 76% vs 52% women / more current smokers / duration higher both categories

----------------------------------------------------------------

[11] EXPOSURE BY SEX  (~0:45)

Before the results, a quick look at exposure size by sex. Sexual assault
involves around 1,100 to 1,200 men, and 3,000 to 3,800 women. Forced
intercourse — the more severe and much rarer form — involves around 260 to 280
men, and roughly 1,100 to 1,500 women. Keep these numbers in mind — they
explain why some confidence intervals are wider than others.

Anchors: assault: ~1,100-1,200 M / 3,000-3,800 F / forced: ~260-280 M / 1,100-1,500 F / explains wider CIs

[14] RESULTS — PERSISTENT SMOKING  (~1:00)
(subtitle on graph: Current smoker vs ex-smoker)

Finally, among people who have ever smoked, are exposed individuals more
likely to still be smoking today, rather than having quit? Here, the picture

repeated, in men. Sexual assault shows no significant association with
difficulty quitting, in either sex.

[15] SUMMARY  (~0:50)

To summarize what we found: smoking is more common among those exposed to
childhood sexual violence, across all violence categories. Exposed individuals
also report a longer cumulative smoking duration. Shorter reported smoking
duration — less than one year versus never smoking — was also associated with
exposure in women specifically. Among those who ever smoked, difficulty
quitting is specifically associated with forced intercourse. The association
tends to be stronger for forced intercourse than for assault, across models.
And this pattern holds for both women and men.

Anchors: more common / longer duration / <1yr vs never — women / difficulty quitting — forced /
tends to be stronger / both sexes

----------------------------------------------------------------

[16] DISCUSSION  (~1:45)

How can we explain this? In our data, exposed individuals are more often
current smokers, report a longer smoking duration, and find it harder to
quit — especially after forced intercourse. This is consistent with a
self-medication explanation. Childhood is a particularly sensitive period:
when something as severe as sexual violence happens at that age, it can leave
lasting traces — it can interfere with brain, neurobiological, and cognitive
development. And in adulthood, that can translate into what we see here:
difficulty regulating emotions, and a stronger pull toward coping mechanisms.
Of course, this study has limitations. It's cross-sectional, so we can't
establish causality. The data is self-reported, with a risk of recall bias.
And statistical power is limited for the rarest exposure categories. As a next
step, we're currently working on longitudinal trajectories, using repeated
measures within the Constances cohort, to help establish temporality.

Anchors: more current, longer, harder to quit / self-medication / sensitive period — lasting traces /
limitations: cross-sectional, recall bias, power / perspective: trajectories

[17] PUBLIC HEALTH RELEVANCE  (~0:55)
(slide closing line: "Childhood sexual violence: an overlooked piece of the smoking
inequality picture — and one small piece of the tobacco endgame ahead.")

Beyond the well-known social gradient in smoking, our findings point to an
overlooked axis of inequality: childhood sexual violence. In France, the
CIIVISE has recommended systematic screening for sexual violence in health
services since 2023 — that's not yet standard practice. On its own, childhood
sexual violence won't explain most of the smoking epidemic. But it's one of
the hidden factors behind why some groups aren't keeping up as overall smoking
rates decline — and closing that gap is one small piece of the tobacco
endgame this conference is working toward.

Anchors: overlooked axis of inequality / CIIVISE 2023 — not yet standard / won't explain most of it /
one small piece of the endgame
